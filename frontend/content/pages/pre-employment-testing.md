# Pre-employment testing: a practical guide for small and mid-size teams

A pre-employment test is a structured check that every applicant for a role takes before you decide who to interview or hire. You may also see it called a pre-employment assessment, a pre-hire assessment or a skills assessment test. The idea is the same: ask everyone the same kind of questions, score them the same way, and compare people on evidence instead of on how their CV reads.

This guide covers the main types of tests, when they help, the fairness and legal basics, and what to look for in a tool. It ends with how prepza does it, and what prepza does not do.

## What a pre-employment test is

A good test checks what the role actually needs, runs under the same conditions for everyone, and gives a result a person can read and explain. It doesn't make the hiring decision; it gives the people who make it better information, earlier.

## Types of pre-employment tests

| Type | What it measures | Typical format | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Skills and job knowledge | Whether someone knows what the job requires | Multiple-choice questions, including reading code; short tasks | Roles with a clear body of knowledge: accounting, sales, support, engineering, marketing |
| Cognitive ability | Reasoning, numeracy, verbal ability | Timed puzzles and problems | Roles where learning speed matters most |
| Personality and behavior | Work style and preferences | Questionnaires | Team fit discussions, development |
| Work samples and job simulations | Doing a slice of the real job | Tasks, case studies, writing code | Late-stage checks for a short list |
| Language and typing | Proficiency and speed | Graded tests | Customer-facing and admin roles |

### Why skills tests are a sensible first step

Large reviews of hiring research rank job-specific measures, such as job knowledge tests, work samples and structured interviews, among the better predictors of job performance on average, and years of experience among the weaker ones. Our guide [Skills tests vs CV screening](/guides/skills-tests-vs-cv-screening) walks through the published numbers and their limits.

Skills tests are also easy to explain: a question about reconciling accounts clearly belongs in a test for an accountant.

## When to use a pre-employment test

Tests help most when:

- **You get more applicants than you can read carefully.** A short test gives every applicant the same kind of chance, instead of only the first 20 CVs you open. See [How to screen 100 applicants in a day](/guides/screen-100-applicants-in-a-day).
- **CVs look alike.** Junior roles, career changers and international applicants are hard to compare on paper.
- **The role has a clear body of knowledge.** If you can list what a new hire must know on day one, you can test it.
- **Interviewers' time is the bottleneck.** Interviews go to people who already showed the basics.

Tests help less when the job depends mainly on things a short test can't show, such as senior leadership judgment, or when you have three applicants and can talk to each one.

Put the test right after applying (through a link in the job ad) or after a quick check of hard requirements such as a license or work permit. Keep it short: a few topics with about 10 questions each.

## Fairness and the law: the basics

This section is a high-level overview as of October 2026, not legal advice. Rules depend on your country, your industry and how you use the results. Check with your own counsel.

### United States: EEOC and the four-fifths rule

US employers who use tests for hiring decisions fall under the federal anti-discrimination laws that the EEOC enforces. The Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures include a rule of thumb for adverse impact: "A selection rate for any race, sex, or ethnic group which is less than four-fifths (4/5) (or eighty percent) of the rate for the group with the highest rate will generally be regarded by the Federal enforcement agencies as evidence of adverse impact" ([29 CFR 1607.4(D)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/1607.4)).

In practice: test only knowledge the job needs, watch pass rates across groups, offer accommodations such as extra time, and check state and city rules for automated hiring tools. For example, New York City's [Local Law 144](https://www.nyc.gov/site/dca/about/automated-employment-decision-tools.page) requires a bias audit, a public summary of it and notice to candidates before an automated employment decision tool is used.

### European Union: the AI Act

The EU AI Act lists AI systems "intended to be used for the recruitment or selection of natural persons, in particular to place targeted job advertisements, to analyse and filter job applications, and to evaluate candidates" as high-risk ([Annex III, point 4(a)](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/)). Tools that score and rank candidates with AI are very likely in scope; for hiring tools, these duties apply from 2 December 2027. High-risk systems carry duties for the vendor (risk management, documentation, logging, human oversight by design, accuracy) and for the employer using them (use as instructed, human oversight, keeping logs, informing candidates).

For the details and the dates, see [Is AI hiring legal in the EU?](/guides/is-ai-hiring-legal-in-the-eu)

### GDPR

Test results are personal data. Under the GDPR (and the UK GDPR, which is similar) you, the employer, are usually the controller, and the testing vendor is your processor. You need a data processing agreement, a clear notice to candidates, a retention limit, and a way to handle access and deletion requests. If a decision is based solely on automated processing and significantly affects a candidate, [Article 22](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-22-gdpr/) applies, so have a person review results. Ask any vendor where data is hosted and which sub-processors it uses.

### The rule that covers all of them

**A person decides.** Use the score to support a decision, not to make it. Look at the answers, not just the number, before you reject anyone.

## How to choose a pre-employment testing tool

Questions worth asking any vendor:

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Can the test match my exact role? | A generic library test may miss half of what your job needs |
| Can I see and change what's tested before candidates take it? | You are responsible for the content |
| How is cheating handled? | Shared questions and open browser tabs weaken any online test |
| What does a candidate see? | Clear instructions and fair time limits reduce drop-off and complaints |
| How is it priced? | A monthly plan can cost more than your whole hiring volume is worth |
| Where is data stored, and for how long? | GDPR, your own policies, candidates' trust |
| What does the vendor say about the AI Act and bias? | You share the duties with them |
| Does it work with your ATS and other tools? | Copying candidates and results by hand costs time and loses applicants |

## How prepza does it

1. **Paste the job description.** Any role, any of 23 languages. prepza picks out what a candidate must know and proposes topics.
2. **Review the topics.** Uncheck what you don't need, rename a topic, edit its subtopics, or describe changes in plain text. Questions are written only after you approve. You can also start from a ready-made template for your role.
3. **Invite candidates.** Send invites by email, paste a list, upload a file, or put one shareable link in your job ad. Reminders go out automatically.
4. **Read the results.** Scores come in ranked, with a scorecard for each candidate.

- **Each candidate gets their own random set** from each topic's bank, with a server-enforced countdown on every question. You can give extra time to anyone who asks.

- **Answers are marked against an answer key** that AI writes; a sample of each topic's keys is checked before release, the rest when a question is flagged. No AI reads or judges a candidate's answer. A PDF report covers one candidate or the whole list.
