# Skills tests vs CV screening: what predicts job performance

Most screening still starts with CVs. A recruiter or hiring manager reads them, looks at job titles, employers and years of experience, and picks who goes forward. It's familiar, and it feels informed. But how well does it predict who will actually do the job well?

Industrial and organizational psychologists have studied this question for decades, by pooling hundreds of studies that compare hiring methods with later job performance. This guide summarizes what two of the most cited reviews found, what the numbers mean, and how to use them without overclaiming.

## The two reviews

**Schmidt and Hunter (1998)** summarized 85 years of research on selection methods in one widely cited article. For years it was the standard reference for "which hiring methods work."

**Sackett, Zhang, Berry and Lievens (2022)**, in the *Journal of Applied Psychology* ([doi:10.1037/apl0000994](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000994)), revisited those estimates. They argued that earlier meta-analyses often over-corrected for a statistical effect called range restriction, which made many methods look more predictive than they are. In their words, "most of the same selection procedures that ranked high in prior summaries remain high in rank, but with mean validity estimates reduced by .10–.20 points."

## What the numbers mean

Both reviews report a **validity** estimate for each method: a correlation between the method's result and later job performance, statistically corrected for things like unreliable performance ratings. 0 means no relationship, 1 a perfect one. In hiring research, values around .30 to .40 are among the best any single method achieves. No method predicts performance precisely; the useful question is which ones predict it better than others.

## The results side by side

| Method | Schmidt & Hunter (1998) | Sackett et al. (2022) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structured interviews | .51 | .42 |
| Job knowledge tests | .48 | .40 |
| Work sample tests | .54 | .33 |
| Cognitive ability tests | .51 | .31 |
| Unstructured interviews | .38 | .19 |
| Job experience (years) | .18 | .07 |

*Values from Table 3 of Sackett et al. (2022), which lists its estimates next to Schmidt and Hunter's (1998).*

A few things stand out:

- **Job knowledge tests rank near the top** in both reviews, second only to structured interviews in the 2022 estimates.
- **Years of experience rank near the bottom.** In the 2022 estimates, years of job experience had a validity of .07, close to no relationship.
- **Structured interviews beat unstructured ones** by a wide margin. The same conversation, with set questions and a scoring guide, predicts far better.
- **Cognitive ability is no longer the clear leader.** The 2022 authors suggest structured interviews, not cognitive tests, as the reference point against which other methods are judged.

The 2022 authors also point out a pattern: the strongest predictors in their re-analysis, "structured interviews, job knowledge tests, empirically keyed biodata, and work samples", "are all job-specific measures."

## What this says about CV screening

CV screening isn't one method, so it doesn't have one validity number. In practice it leans heavily on signals like years of experience, past job titles and education. Years of experience, at least, is one of the weakest predictors in both reviews. Schmidt and Hunter also list years of education at .10.

CVs are also hard to compare. Two people with the same knowledge can write very different CVs, and a strong CV can reflect good writing as much as good work. An unstructured read of a CV has the same problem as an unstructured interview: different readers notice different things.

That doesn't make CVs useless. They're the right place to check hard requirements (a license, a work permit, a language) and to prepare questions for an interview. The research suggests they are a weak tool for ranking people.

## Why "job-specific" matters

The job knowledge result comes with an important condition. Sackett et al. relied on a meta-analysis by Dye et al. (1993), which separated knowledge tests built for the job in question from tests of knowledge unrelated to it. Of the studies found, the 59 that used job-specific knowledge tests had a higher mean observed validity (.31) than the full set of 164 (.22). The .40 estimate is for job-specific tests.

In plain terms: a knowledge test helps when it tests what the job actually needs. A generic test that doesn't fit the role predicts less.

The authors also note a second condition: work samples and knowledge tests suit hiring where candidates are expected to already have training or experience. For roles where you'll teach everything on the job, a knowledge test of the job's content is less appropriate.

## Limits to keep in mind

- **These are averages across many jobs and studies.** Your role, your test and your candidates may differ.
- **Validity estimates are for well-built methods.** A badly written test or a sloppy interview won't reach these numbers.
- **One method is rarely enough.** Researchers generally advise combining methods, for example a knowledge test early and a structured interview later.
- **Fairness needs its own checks.** Sackett et al. pair validity with average score differences between Black and White candidates. Job knowledge tests, work samples and cognitive tests show substantial differences; structured interviews show smaller ones. Combining methods can reduce them. Whatever you use, monitor pass rates across groups. In the US, the four-fifths rule of thumb in the [Uniform Guidelines](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/1607.4) is the usual starting point.
- **A score supports a decision; it doesn't make it.** A person should look at the answers before deciding.

## A screening process that uses the research

1. **Check hard requirements from the CV.** Yes-or-no only: license, right to work, required language.
2. **Give everyone left a short job-specific knowledge test.** Same topics, same time limits, the same rules for everyone.
3. **Review the top results carefully,** including answers, not just scores.
4. **Run structured interviews** with the short list: the same questions for each candidate and a simple scoring guide.
5. **Add a work sample** for the final few if the role allows it.
