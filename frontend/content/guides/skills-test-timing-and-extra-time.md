# How to time a skills test: question timers, length and extra time

A skills test with good questions can still give poor results. Too much time and candidates look up every answer; too little and you measure reading speed. Too few questions and the score swings on luck; too many and good candidates give up halfway.

This guide covers four settings of a timed multiple-choice test: the time per question, the number of questions, the total length, and extra time for candidates who need it. The advice applies to any timed knowledge test; the last section shows how testee handles each one.

## The short version

| Setting | A sensible default | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time per question | About 60 seconds; up to 75–90 for senior-level questions | Enough to read and think, not enough to search |
| Questions per topic | About 10 | Enough for a stable topic score, short enough to finish |
| Topics | 3 to 5 core requirements | Each one a real must-have from the job |
| Total length | 20 to 40 minutes | Long enough to be reliable, short enough to finish in one sitting |
| Extra time | +25%, +50% or +100%, per candidate, on request | The same test, with the time a person needs to show what they know |

## 1. Time per question

### Why a timer on each question

You can time a whole test with one clock, or put a countdown on each question. For a short knowledge screen, the countdown per question wins.

- **The same conditions for everyone.** Every candidate gets the same time on every question. Nobody spends 15 minutes on question 3 and rushes the rest.
- **It measures what people know.** Someone who knows a topic recognizes the right answer quickly. Someone who doesn't needs to look it up, and a per-question timer leaves little room for that.
- **It keeps the test short and predictable.** Candidates know exactly how long it takes before they start, and so do you.

### How much time

Write questions so a capable person can read the question and all the options and answer in about a minute, also in a second language. The hardest, with several steps of reasoning, can need up to a minute and a half. Then set the timer to match: around 60 seconds for most tests, 75 to 90 for a test written for senior people.

### Why not more

Every extra minute is a minute to search the web, paste the question into an AI chat or message a friend. Past a point, a knowledge test becomes a test of how fast someone finds answers, and it rewards whoever is most willing to bend the rules.

A tight timer only works with short questions. A long question on a tight timer measures reading speed, which is unfair to people reading in a second language and to people with dyslexia. Keep questions to one or two sentences, options short, and examples to the lines the answer depends on.

### What happens when time runs out

Decide it up front and tell candidates before they start. A common rule: the pick on screen counts, and with no pick the question is wrong. Unanswered questions count as wrong too, so leaving the test gains nothing.

## 2. How many questions

### More questions, more reliable scores

Each question is a small sample of what someone knows: a lucky guess or one oddly worded item can move the score. More questions even those accidents out. Test theory's Spearman–Brown formula describes this: lengthening a test with similar questions makes its scores more reliable, with smaller gains as it grows. As an illustration: if a 10-question topic has a reliability of .60, doubling it to 20 questions raises it to about .75, while halving it to 5 drops it to about .43. The exact numbers depend on the questions; the direction doesn't.

### But not so many that people quit

Length has costs too: fatigue, dropping attention, and candidates with other offers who don't finish. A practical balance:

- **About 10 questions per topic,** enough to tell who knows a topic from who doesn't.
- **More on the topics that matter most.** If one skill decides the hire, give it 15 or 20.
- **Fewer topics, not fewer questions per topic.** Four topics of 10 questions tell you more than eight of 5. Keep "nice to have" skills for later conversations.

### Draw from a larger bank

If everyone gets the same 30 questions, the answers get shared. A larger bank per topic, with a different random set for each candidate, avoids that. Scores from different sets aren't exactly comparable, so treat small differences as noise and look at the answers, not only the totals.

## 3. Total length

The total follows from the first two settings: questions times seconds. Three topics of 10 questions at 60 seconds is at most 30 minutes; most candidates will finish faster, because they answer before the timer runs out.

For a first screen, 20 to 40 minutes works well. Shorter, and there are usually too few questions per topic to trust the scores; much longer, and it starts to feel like a second job application.

Tell candidates the time per question, the total and what happens when time runs out, in the invite or job ad and again before they start, so they can set the time aside.

## 4. Extra time for candidates who need it

A timer is fair only if it measures the same thing for everyone. A person with dyslexia, a visual impairment, a condition that affects processing speed or another disability may know the material well and still need longer to read and answer. Extra time doesn't make the test easier; it removes an obstacle that has nothing to do with the job.

### Handling requests well

- **Tell every candidate they can ask,** and how, before they start.
- **Grant it per candidate,** on their personal invite, so it applies to that person only.
- **Use a few standard amounts,** such as +25%, +50% and +100% of each question's time. They're easier to apply consistently than minutes decided case by case.
- **Ask only what you need.** For a short screening test, "I need extra time" is usually enough. Don't ask for a diagnosis or medical records; if your organization needs some evidence, ask for as little as possible and keep it apart from hiring decisions.
- **Keep it private.** The people deciding on the hire don't need to know why a candidate had extra time.
- **Set it before the test starts,** not halfway through.
- **Treat the score the same way.** Don't discount a score earned with extra time.

### What about candidates reading in a second language?

Short, plain questions do most of the work here. Whether you also give extra time for language is your decision: if the job needs fluent reading in that language, the timer may be part of what you're testing; if it doesn't, extra time on request is reasonable. Either way, apply the same rule to everyone.

### Your legal obligations

Many countries require employers to make reasonable adjustments for disabled applicants, including in tests. Examples include the EU's Employment Equality Directive ([Directive 2000/78/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2000/78/oj), Article 5), the UK's Equality Act 2010 ([section 20](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/20)) and, in the US, the Americans with Disabilities Act, whose regulations specifically cover how tests are given to applicants with disabilities ([29 CFR 1630.11](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/1630.11)). National rules also limit what you may ask about health before a job offer.

**This section is practical guidance, not legal advice.** What you must offer, and what you may ask, depends on where you hire. Check your own obligations under the equality and disability law that applies to you.

## How testee handles timing and extra time

In testee, an interview is a timed multiple-choice skills test created from your job description.

### Time per question

- **Every question has its own countdown,** kept by the server, so closing the tab or reloading the page doesn't stop it.
- **The default depends on the interview's level,** which for a generated interview comes from the seniority the job description asks for: 60 seconds for a basic or medium interview, 75 seconds for a hard one.
- **This is an average: each question gets its own time** from it when a candidate's questions are drawn, by how long it takes to read and answer: its words, any code or example (read more slowly), and time to work out the answer. Short questions get less, longer ones and code get more, each between half and twice the average, and together they add up to exactly the number of questions times the average. The countdown shows each question's own time, and the scorecard shows how long each answer took out of it.
- **You can change the average per interview,** in its settings, from 10 to 600 seconds. A change applies to candidates who start after it; a candidate's time is fixed when they start.
- **When time runs out,** the pick on screen counts and the next question opens; with no pick, the question counts as wrong. Unanswered questions count as wrong, and an interview a candidate leaves finishes by itself once its total time, plus 10%, has passed.
- **Questions are written to fit the timer:** answered in about a minute (up to a minute and a half for the hardest), also in a second language. A question is at most 40 words (50 for a hard one), an option at most 10, and an example at most 8 short lines.
- **Questions that run out of time too often are flagged** and rewritten in the background. A rewritten question applies to candidates who start after the fix; results already given don't change.
- **Answer times are also an integrity signal.** When a finished candidate's times stay about the same on every question, whatever its length or difficulty, their report points it out: relaying questions to an AI tends to look like that. It's a reason to ask, not proof, and extra time doesn't trigger it.

In an interview in Arabic, Chinese, Japanese, Persian, Russian, Thai or Ukrainian, every question gets the average, as a question's length in these languages doesn't yet predict its time well enough. About 1 in 10 are "select all that apply", with 2 or 3 right options, earning part of the point for each right option ticked and nothing if a wrong one is ticked.

### Number of questions

- **Each topic of a generated interview has a bank of 70 questions,** and an interview has at most 10 topics, which you approve before any question is written.
- **Each candidate gets 10 questions per topic by default,** a random set from the bank in their own order, with the same share of "select all that apply" questions. You can set a different number for each topic. Set it to 0 to leave a topic out; at least one topic keeps its questions.

### Total length

Before starting, candidates see the average time per question and the whole interview's time in minutes, rounded up: the questions they get from every topic, times the average seconds per question. With the defaults, three topics take at most 30 minutes, or 38 on a hard interview. On the interview's topics tab you see the total questions, the average time per question and the whole interview's time, updated as you change them.

### Extra time

- **Owners and admins can give a candidate +25%, +50% or +100%** of each question's time, on the candidate's page.
- **Only before the candidate starts.** After that the time is fixed, and the candidate's page still shows the extra time they had.
- **No reason is recorded.** The company's action log records only the change.
- **Candidates see their own time:** the average time per question and the total, extra time included, then each question's own countdown, and the start page tells every candidate they can ask for more.
- **Shareable-link candidates** can't be given extra time on the link itself, because their invite is made when they start. The link's page tells them to ask you for a personal invite before they start.

Before inviting anyone, take the interview yourself from its page: it's free, doesn't count as a candidate, and shows quickly whether the timer feels right.
