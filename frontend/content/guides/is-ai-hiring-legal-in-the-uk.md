# Is AI hiring legal in the UK? What employers need to know

In general, yes: using AI in hiring is legal in the UK. The UK has no single AI law like the EU's AI Act. Instead, the Equality Act 2010 and the UK GDPR apply to AI tools as they do to any test, and since 5 February 2026 the UK GDPR has new rules on decisions made solely by automated means.

This guide explains the rules at a high level, what employers have to do, and how testee approaches it. **It is not legal advice.** It reflects the rules as of October 2026; some of them are new and the regulator's guidance on them is still being updated.

## The short answer

| Question | Short answer |
| --- | --- |
| Can I use AI to screen or evaluate candidates? | Yes, within the Equality Act 2010 and the UK GDPR |
| Can a decision be made by the tool alone? | Only with safeguards, and almost never on special category data |
| When did the new automated-decision rules apply? | From 5 February 2026 |
| Do I have to tell candidates? | Yes: what data is processed, how and why, and about any solely automated decision |
| Do I have to offer extra time? | To disabled candidates who need it: a reasonable adjustment |
| Who makes the hiring decision? | A person, ideally. It is the simplest way to stay outside the strictest rules |

## The Equality Act 2010

The [Equality Act 2010](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/contents) applies to a skills test as it does to an interview:

- **Direct discrimination:** treating a candidate less favourably because of a protected characteristic, such as sex, race, age or disability.
- **Indirect discrimination:** a test that applies to everyone but puts a group at a particular disadvantage, unless you can show it's a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim. Testing knowledge the job doesn't need, or a passing grade set higher than the job requires, is where this risk lies.
- **Reasonable adjustments:** you must make reasonable adjustments for disabled candidates ([section 20](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/20)). For a timed test, the usual one is extra time; another is a different way to answer or a different assessment.

Who wrote the test doesn't matter: the employer answers for the way it selects candidates.

## Automated decisions: what the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 changed

The old Article 22 of the UK GDPR gave people the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, with a few exceptions. [Section 80 of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/section/80) replaced it with new Articles 22A to 22D, in force since 5 February 2026 ([SI 2026/82](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/82/regulation/2/made), regulation 2(j)).

**What counts.** Under Article 22A, a decision is "based solely on automated processing if there is no meaningful human involvement in the taking of the decision", and it is significant if it has a legal or "similarly significant effect" on the person. Rejecting a candidate, or deciding who moves on, is very likely significant. Whether a person's involvement is meaningful depends on what they actually do, including "the extent to which the decision is reached by means of profiling."

**When it's allowed.** Solely automated significant decisions are now allowed in more cases than before, on any lawful basis, provided the safeguards below are in place. The stricter rule is for special category data, such as health, ethnicity or religion (Article 22B): a significant decision based even partly on it may be taken solely by automated means only with the candidate's explicit consent, or where it is needed for a contract or required by law and a substantial public interest condition applies. A decision relying on the new "recognised legitimate interests" basis may not be solely automated at all.

**The safeguards.** Under [Article 22C](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/22C), a controller taking such decisions must have measures that:

- "provide the data subject with information about decisions" taken about them;
- "enable the data subject to make representations about such decisions";
- "enable the data subject to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller";
- "enable the data subject to contest such decisions."

Article 22D lets the government add to these rules by regulations, for example on what counts as meaningful human involvement. Decisions made before 5 February 2026 stay under the old Article 22.

**In practice:** have a person who understands the results and can overrule them look at each one before a candidate is rejected, and tell candidates how to ask for a review. Then the decision isn't solely automated, and the safeguards are good practice rather than a legal requirement.

## The UK GDPR basics

Candidate data falls under the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018, which work much like the EU's GDPR:

- **Roles.** Your company is usually the controller of candidate data; a testing vendor is your processor. Sign a data processing agreement that covers UK law.
- **Notice.** Tell candidates that AI is used, what data is processed, how and why, and how long you keep it.
- **Data protection impact assessment.** The ICO treats AI in recruitment as high risk: run a DPIA before you start.
- **Collect only what you need,** and keep it no longer than you need it.
- **Rights.** Candidates can ask for access to their data, for corrections and for deletion.

## What the ICO found when it audited AI recruitment tools

In November 2024 the Information Commissioner's Office published the outcomes of its audits of providers of AI sourcing, screening and selection tools. It made 296 recommendations and 42 advisory notes, and says "Both AI providers and recruiters should follow the recommendations in this report." What it found:

- **Inferred characteristics.** Some tools "estimated or inferred people's gender, ethnicity, and other characteristics from their job application or even just their name, rather than asking candidates directly." The ICO's view: "This inferred information is not accurate enough to monitor bias effectively." It was often processed without a lawful basis and without the candidate's knowledge.
- **Filtering by protected characteristics.** Some tools let recruiters filter out candidates with certain protected characteristics.
- **Too much data.** Some tools "collected far more personal information than was needed," and some combined it with data scraped from job networking sites and social media.
- **Unclear roles.** Several providers called themselves processors when they were in fact controllers, and tried to pass all responsibility to recruiters through vague contracts.
- **Good practice too:** many providers monitored their tools' accuracy and bias, and some published detailed information about how their AI works.

Its seven key recommendations, for providers and recruiters alike:

1. **Fairness.** Monitor the tool and its outputs for fairness, accuracy and bias, and act on what you find. Any data used to monitor bias must be accurate enough: "Inferred or estimated data will not be adequate and accurate enough."
2. **Transparency and explainability.** Tell candidates what personal information the AI processes and how, the logic behind its outputs, and whether their data is used to train it. Agree in the contract who tells them.
3. **Data minimisation and purpose limitation.** Collect only the minimum needed, and don't reuse it for another purpose.
4. **DPIAs.** Complete one before processing, and update it as the tool or the processing changes.
5. **Controller and processor roles.** Define them for each processing and record them in contracts and privacy information.
6. **Explicit processing instructions.** Give the provider written instructions (the data fields, the purposes, the outputs, the safeguards), and check it follows them.
7. **Lawful basis.** Identify one before processing, plus a condition for any special category data.

The ICO also recommends meaningful human reviews of AI outputs, with records of the reviews done.

## Transfers to the US

If a provider sends UK candidates' data to the United States, the UK–US data bridge can cover it: [The Data Protection (Adequacy) (United States of America) Regulations 2023](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2023/1028/contents), in force since 12 October 2023, for US companies certified under the UK Extension to the EU–US Data Privacy Framework. Others need safeguards, such as standard contractual clauses with the UK addendum.

## A practical checklist for employers

1. **List the AI tools in your hiring,** including features inside your ATS, and what each one decides.
2. **Test only what the job needs.** Choose topics and a passing grade you can justify for the role.
3. **Keep a person in each decision,** with the knowledge and authority to overrule the result.
4. **Update your candidate notice** to say AI is used, how results are used, and how to ask for a reasonable adjustment and a human review.
5. **Offer reasonable adjustments,** such as extra time, and agree them before the candidate starts.
6. **Run or update your DPIA.**
7. **Ask each provider** whether it is your processor or a controller, whether it infers any characteristics, and how it monitors bias.
8. **Monitor fairness** on your own outcomes with data candidates gave you themselves, never inferred data.
9. **Never reject on a score alone.** Look at the answers.

## How testee approaches this

testee runs timed multiple-choice skills interviews written by AI from a job description. Its approach in the UK is the same as in the EU. testee does not claim to be compliant or certified.

What that looks like in the product today:

- **AI writes the questions and their answer keys; marking follows fixed rules.** No AI reads or judges a candidate's answer, and no video or face analysis is used. In voice mode, speech is turned into text and matched to an option, which is said back to the candidate and shown on screen: one clearly named option is saved at once, and a choice of several options is confirmed first. Speech is never stored and the voice is never analysed.
- **You approve the topics** before any question is written, and you choose the passing grade.
- **People decide.** The results page and PDF reports remind your team to review results; testee doesn't reject candidates or send rejection messages.
- **Candidates are told** before they start that questions are written by AI, that people at your company review results and decide, and that they can ask for a review.
- **No protected characteristics.** testee never asks for, infers or stores candidates' sex, ethnicity, age, disability or other protected characteristics.
- **Fairness monitoring.** Your company's owner can download a bias-audit file from the company's Action log page: each finished candidate's grade, the passing grade, whether they passed and whether they scored above the interview's median, keyed by email. Join it with the answers candidates gave you on your own equal opportunities form, then remove the email.
- **Extra time** of +25%, +50% or +100% on every question can be given to any candidate who asks, before they start. testee stores only the amount, never the reason.
- **Documents.** Candidate notice templates, the instructions for companies and a DPIA template are on the [documents page](/documents).
- **Data.** Your company is the controller and testee the processor, under the [data processing agreement](/dpa), which covers UK law. Data is hosted in the EU and isn't used to train AI models; transfers of UK candidates' data to US sub-processors rely on the UK–US data bridge or the UK addendum (see the [privacy policy](/privacy)). Candidate data is deleted 12 months after the invite.
