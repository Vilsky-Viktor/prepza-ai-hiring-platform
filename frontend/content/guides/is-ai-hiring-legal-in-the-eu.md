# Is AI hiring legal in the EU? What employers need to know

In general, yes: using AI in hiring is legal in the EU. But most AI tools that evaluate candidates fall into the AI Act's high-risk category, which brings duties for the vendor and for you, the employer. A few practices are banned outright. And the GDPR applies to candidate data, as it always has.

This guide explains the rules at a high level, the dates that matter, what employers have to do, and how prepza approaches it. **It is not legal advice.** It reflects the rules as of October 2026. The rules are new, guidance is still being finalized, and national law adds its own requirements. Check with your own counsel before relying on any of this.

## The short answer

| Question | Short answer |
| --- | --- |
| Can I use AI to screen or evaluate candidates? | Yes, within the rules |
| Is a hiring tool that scores candidates "high-risk" under the AI Act? | Very likely, under Annex III, point 4(a) |
| When do the high-risk rules apply? | From 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems |
| Is anything banned already? | Yes, for example AI that infers emotions in the workplace, since 2 February 2025 |
| Does the GDPR still apply? | Yes, to all candidate data |
| Who makes the hiring decision? | A person. The rules are built around human oversight |

## The AI Act's high-risk category for hiring

The AI Act lists the following as high-risk in [Annex III, point 4(a)](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/):

> AI systems intended to be used for the recruitment or selection of natural persons, in particular to place targeted job advertisements, to analyse and filter job applications, and to evaluate candidates.

That covers a wide range of tools: CV filters, ranking systems, AI interviewers, and tests that score candidates with AI involved.

### Does a human making the final decision change that?

Usually not. [Article 6(3)](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/6/) of the AI Act lets some Annex III systems out of the high-risk category, for example when they perform a narrow procedural task or only prepare a human assessment. But an Annex III system that profiles people is always high-risk, and scoring or ranking candidates on their abilities is likely to count as profiling.

The European Commission's draft guidelines on high-risk classification (published 19 May 2026 for consultation; not final as of October 2026) take a broad view. They describe selection as covering "shortlisting, grading, ranking, or testing of candidates", and, as summarized by [Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/05/ai-act-update), treat systems that generate suitability scores as high-risk "even if the final hiring decision is made by a human recruiter."

So a tool that gives each candidate a score and a ranking is very likely high-risk, even though people decide who gets hired.

## The dates

| Date | What applies |
| --- | --- |
| 2 February 2025 | Prohibited practices (Article 5) and AI literacy (Article 4) |
| 2 August 2026 | Most of the remaining rules, including transparency for some AI systems (Article 50) |
| 2 December 2027 | High-risk obligations for Annex III systems, including hiring tools |
| 2 August 2028 | High-risk obligations for AI in products covered by Annex I |

The high-risk date for Annex III was originally 2 August 2026. The Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force since 27 July 2026) moved it to 2 December 2027 ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/eu-digital-omnibus-ai-enters-force)). Dates as shown on the [AI Act Explorer, Article 113](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/113/), as of October 2026.

The extra time is for preparation, not a reason to ignore the rules. The GDPR and anti-discrimination laws already apply in full.

## What's banned

Some AI practices are prohibited, whatever the risk category. Most relevant for hiring: "the use of AI systems to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions, except where the use of the AI system is intended to be put in place or into the market for medical or safety reasons" ([Article 5(1)(f)](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/5/)).

If a video interview tool claims to read candidates' enthusiasm, stress or honesty from their face or voice, ask hard questions before using it in the EU.

## What the vendor (provider) must do

For a high-risk system, the provider carries most of the technical burden. By the application date, it needs:

- A risk management system (Article 9)
- Data governance, including checks for bias (Article 10)
- Technical documentation (Article 11)
- Automatic logging (Article 12)
- Instructions for use for employers (Article 13)
- Human oversight built into the design (Article 14)
- Accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity (Article 15)
- A quality management system (Article 17)
- A conformity assessment, an EU declaration of conformity and CE marking
- Registration in the EU database (Article 49)
- Post-market monitoring and serious-incident reporting (Articles 72 and 73)

For hiring tools, the provider assesses conformity itself (internal control); no notified body is needed.

## What you, the employer (deployer), must do

Article 26 sets the employer's duties. In summary ([Article 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/)):

- **Use the system as instructed,** following the provider's instructions for use.
- **Assign human oversight** to people "who have the necessary competence, training and authority, as well as the necessary support."
- **Monitor the system** and report risks and serious incidents to the provider.
- **Keep the logs** the system produces for at least six months, unless other law says otherwise.
- **Inform workers' representatives and affected workers** before using a high-risk system at work.
- **Tell candidates** that they are subject to the use of a high-risk AI system.

Public bodies and some private organizations must also run a fundamental rights impact assessment (Article 27). And the AI literacy duty in Article 4 already applies: the people using AI tools in your hiring should understand what those tools do and don't do.

## The GDPR basics

Candidate data is personal data, and AI doesn't change the GDPR. The essentials:

- **Roles.** You are usually the controller of candidate data; a testing vendor is your processor. Sign a data processing agreement.
- **Notice.** Tell candidates what you collect, why, how long you keep it and their rights.
- **Data protection impact assessment.** Systematic evaluation of candidates with new technology usually calls for one.
- **Retention.** Set a limit and stick to it.
- **Rights.** Candidates can ask for access to their data, for corrections and for deletion.
- **Automated decisions.** [Article 22 of the GDPR](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-22-gdpr/) gives people "the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling", where it has legal or similarly significant effects. Rejecting candidates purely on a score, with no person reviewing, can fall under it. Have a person look at each result before acting on it.

## A practical checklist for employers

1. **List the AI tools in your hiring,** including features inside your ATS.
2. **Ask each vendor** whether they consider their tool high-risk, and what documentation they will provide by December 2027.
3. **Check for banned practices,** especially emotion inference from video or voice.
4. **Name the people** who review AI outputs, and make sure they understand them.
5. **Update your candidate notice** to mention AI use and how to ask for a human review.
6. **Inform workers' representatives** where required.
7. **Run or update your DPIA.**
8. **Offer accommodations,** such as extra time, to candidates who need them.
9. **Never reject on a score alone.** Look at the answers.

## How prepza approaches this

prepza runs timed multiple-choice skills interviews written by AI from a job description. Because it scores and ranks candidates, prepza's own analysis concludes that it is very likely high-risk under Annex III, point 4(a), with obligations applying from 2 December 2027. It is preparing on that basis. prepza does not claim to be compliant or certified.

What that looks like in the product today:

- **AI writes the questions and their answer keys; marking follows fixed rules.** No AI reads or judges a candidate's answer, and no video, voice or face analysis is used. If AI corrects a key later, the fix applies to candidates who start after it; past results don't change.
- **You approve the topics** before any question is written, and you can see every question.
- **People decide.** The results page and PDF reports remind your team to review results; prepza doesn't reject candidates or send rejection messages.
- **Candidates are told** before they start that questions are written by AI, that people at your company review results and decide, and that they can ask for a review.
- **Extra time** can be given to any candidate who asks.
- **Records.** The questions shown, answers, timings and integrity events are recorded.
- **Data.** Hosted in the EU (some sub-processors are in the US; see the [privacy policy](/privacy)), candidate data deleted 12 months after the invite, a data processing agreement included, and your data isn't used to train AI models.

Drafts of the technical documentation, risk register, instructions for companies, quality management system and post-market monitoring plan exist and are being completed ahead of the 2027 date. They are working drafts, not a claim of compliance.
